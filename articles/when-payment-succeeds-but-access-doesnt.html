When Payment Succeeds
but Access Doesn't

A real subscription incident, traced from a successful Stripe payment to a missing Premium entitlement—and the checks that confirmed the fix.

Failure pointWebhook to database
RecoveryReplay and verify

Audience
SaaS product & engineering teams
Focus
Payment-to-entitlement reliability

The payment had gone through. The account still showed the free plan.

I noticed the mismatch while running Magical Chart, my subscription product. A customer had completed checkout, but the Premium access their payment was meant to unlock had not appeared. From the customer’s point of view, the important part was simple: they had paid and could not use what they bought.

I’m keeping the account and payment details private. The useful lesson is in the gap between the billing event and the product state: a successful checkout is not the same as a completed activation.

The symptom was clear; the cause was downstream

At first, there were two facts to reconcile: Stripe had recorded a successful payment, while the application still treated the account as free. That narrowed the investigation. The checkout itself had completed; the state change inside the product had not.

In a subscription application, those states often live in different systems. A payment provider processes billing, then sends an event to the application. The application uses that event to update its own record of what an account can access. If that handoff fails, the payment can be valid while the product remains out of sync.

That was the failure here: the Stripe webhook had not successfully updated the user’s entitlement in the database. I did not need to guess whether the customer had paid. I needed to follow the event that should have translated that payment into access.

Following the event to the missing state change

I traced the relevant webhook event and the path from receipt to the database update. The key question was not merely whether an event existed, but whether the application had processed it into the account state the product reads when deciding access.

That distinction matters because webhook delivery and application processing are separate steps. A provider can send an event while the receiving endpoint rejects it, errors during handling, or fails before persisting the result. A green payment status alone does not prove the entitlement was written.

I found the broken connection between the webhook handling and the database update. Once I corrected it, I resent the failed event and checked the account in the product. Premium access appeared as expected. I then verified the full path end to end so the fix was confirmed in the experience the customer actually uses—not inferred from a successful response in a log.

What I checked before calling it fixed

  1. The source event: confirm the billing provider recorded the relevant successful payment or subscription event.
  2. The receiver: confirm the application endpoint accepted and processed the webhook, rather than relying on the checkout redirect.
  3. The persisted state: inspect the account entitlement in the database and verify it matches the subscription state.
  4. The user experience: load the account in the product and confirm the paid feature is actually available.
  5. The recovery: replay the failed event only after correcting the cause, then verify the resulting state again.

Each check answers a different question. Provider records establish what happened to the payment. Webhook handling explains whether the application received the signal. The database shows what the product believes. The final in-product check confirms the customer-facing outcome.

Why replaying an event needs care

Replaying a webhook is useful for recovery, but it should not create duplicate effects. Providers can retry delivery, and operators can resend events. Application handlers should therefore be designed to tolerate the same event arriving more than once. In practice, that means tracking processed event identifiers or making state updates safely repeatable.

The broader reliability principle is to treat incoming events as messages that may be retried. Verify their signatures, record enough information to investigate failures, return a successful response only when handling is complete, and make retries safe. Stripe’s webhook documentation describes delivery and retry behavior; the implementation details depend on the application’s architecture.

The metric I would watch now

A checkout conversion rate would not have exposed this incident by itself. The purchase succeeded. The failure happened after payment, when the product should have granted access.

For a subscription product, I would monitor the share of confirmed paid or activated subscriptions whose matching entitlement becomes active within a defined interval. I would also alert on webhook processing failures and compare provider subscription state with the application’s entitlement state. These checks connect the billing system to the outcome customers care about.

The exact interval and alert threshold should fit the product’s architecture and normal processing time. The important part is to measure both sides of the handoff and investigate meaningful mismatches, rather than treating a payment confirmation as the finish line.

A small incident with a useful boundary

This was a contained failure in my own product, not a claim that every payment problem comes from webhooks. Checkout configuration, subscription state, database writes, caching and account matching can all produce different symptoms. The first step is to identify which system has the authoritative record for each part of the journey, then compare those records in order.

In this case, the customer had paid, the application had not granted access, and the broken link was the webhook-to-database update. Correcting that connection, replaying the failed event and verifying the account restored the expected state.

It also changed how I think about “payment success.” For the person buying, the transaction is not complete when a provider says it succeeded. It is complete when the product reflects the purchase and the promised access works.

About this case: This account is based on a real incident in my own subscription product. Customer identifiers, payment amounts and dates are omitted. The incident involved a failed Stripe webhook update to the entitlement record; no wider outage or financial loss is implied.
