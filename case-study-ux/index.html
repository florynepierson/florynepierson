Why satisfied users
don't return

A research study on the gap between satisfaction and retention in a live GenAI product — and how three measurement errors had to be corrected before any real finding could be trusted.

Product Magical Chart — AI-powered astrology & tarot SaaS
My role Solo founder — research, product, analytics, engineering
Methods Behavioral analytics, funnel analysis, in-product ratings, unsolicited user feedback
01 · Context

The product and the question

Magical Chart is a freemium AI tarot and astrology product. Free users can get one reading per day; a paid subscription unlocks unlimited readings, a 165-lesson course, and additional reports. The core product loop is: visit → free reading → return → subscribe.

After several months of live traffic, the data showed a stable pattern I couldn't explain: the people who paid rated the product 5 out of 5. And they still didn't come back.

That is the research question: if satisfaction is high, why is retention near zero?

Initial hypothesis

My first assumption was an activation problem: most visitors never started a reading, so they never experienced the product. Fix activation, fix retention. The data invalidated this — and finding out why took correcting three instrumentation errors first.

02 · Before the findings: fixing the data

Three measurements that were wrong

Before any finding could be trusted, I had to identify and correct three instrumentation errors that were each producing a plausible but false picture.

Error 1 — The database row cap

The metrics endpoint returned exactly 1,000 readings per query and treated that as a total. The real 30-day volume was more than double. Everything built on "usage" before this was corrected was measuring an artifact, not behavior.

Error 2 — Activation event scope

The start_reading event only fired on the homepage tool. The product runs across 126 pages, most of which were invisible to that metric. The 5% activation rate I had been acting on mixed two completely different populations: homepage visitors (36% activation) and content page visitors (1.7%). Treating them as one number had led to the wrong intervention for two months.

Error 3 — Bot traffic

A spike in readings that looked like a growth signal turned out to be automated traffic. The signature: several languages disappeared overnight while real traffic kept rising, and the endpoint showed zero errors. Detecting this avoided misreading a robot as a user cohort. It also revealed that the bot was consuming more than half the monthly AI cost.

⚑

The method that worked: cross-referencing two independent sources (GA4 events and AI API billing, which cannot be faked) before trusting any single number. When they disagreed, I investigated the gap rather than picking the more convenient figure.

03 · Findings

What the data said

<2% Week-1 return rate — the share of new users still active 7 days after their first visit
5 / 5 Average in-product rating from paying users — every rating submitted was maximum score
36% Activation on the homepage — users who start a reading after landing there
1.7% Activation on content pages — the same action, same product, different entry point

Finding 1 — Satisfaction does not predict retention

Users who completed a paid reading gave it the highest possible rating and did not return. The product delivered on its promise within a single session. The issue is not quality; it is the absence of a compelling reason to come back tomorrow.

Finding 2 — The bottleneck is at the offer click, not the checkout

Once a user reached the checkout page, 20% converted to a purchase. The drop happened much earlier: only 2.7% of users who saw an offer clicked it. The friction is in communicating value, not in completing payment.

Finding 3 — Two audiences, one product

Traffic analysis by keyword intent and geography revealed two populations with almost no overlap: one arriving via spiritual/esoteric queries (high volume, low purchase intent, concentrated in South Asia), and one arriving via self-development queries (lower volume, measurable purchase intent, US-dominant). These two groups were landing on the same pages with the same offer — and reading completely different signals from it.

04 · Qualitative signal

The unsolicited email

Among the users who cancelled, one sent an unsolicited message that reframed the entire retention problem:

"I came across your page wanting confirmation that I had made a right decision about something, but I realised just because I questioned a decision didn't mean it was wrong. I canceled simply because I want to live in the moment and trust my intuition more. Your site is incredible, I honestly enjoyed all the features — it has depth, and it doesn't feel manipulative. For me it was a 1000% experience, no complaints whatsoever. You built a great thing."
Paying subscriber, unsolicited email after cancellation

This introduced a concept I had not framed before: graduation churn. Some users leave not because the product failed, but because it succeeded — they reached the clarity they came for and no longer needed an external mirror. The product's own positioning ("not to predict, to pivot") was creating self-resolving sessions.

This does not make retention impossible, but it redefines what retention means for this product: the goal is not to create dependency, but to give people a reason to return when the next moment of doubt arrives.

05 · Decisions taken

What changed

  • 1
    Stopped redesigning the homepage. Activation at 36% was not the problem. Redirected effort to the content pages, which accounted for 80% of traffic and had 1.7% activation — fixing the bridge between article and tool was worth 20× more than homepage polish.
  • 2
    Reframed the CRO target. The checkout was not the leak. The intervention needed to be on offer visibility and the moment before the offer click — not on simplifying payment flow.
  • 3
    Separated the two audiences in content and acquisition strategy. Building pages that mix high-volume/low-intent and low-volume/high-intent traffic on the same CTA actively suppresses conversion for both groups.
  • 4
    Reframed retention from "daily habit" to "return when it matters." The product's ethical positioning (non-manipulative, autonomy-first) makes compulsion loops both unethical and ineffective. The alternative is a product strong enough that users remember it when they need it next — which is a content and trust problem, not a push notification problem.
06 · Impact

What changed

The activation rate on content pages moved from 1.7% toward the homepage baseline (36%) after the content-to-tool bridge was reworked — the gap narrowed, though it has not closed. The offer click rate remains under 5%, which confirms the CRO work is ongoing.

The more lasting impact was on how decisions get made: no product change is now justified without a named data source. The two-source cross-check (events + API billing) is now standard before any metric is acted on.

Note: this product is an independent side project, not a team environment. Retention numbers remain low in absolute terms. The value of this case study is not the outcome metrics but the research process — finding the right question, correcting the instrumentation, and acting on the finding rather than the assumption.

Reflection

The main methodological lesson: a plausible number is not a reliable number. Every metric I corrected looked reasonable before correction. Cross-referencing independent data sources — especially one that reflects real cost (API billing) versus one that reflects declared events (analytics) — is the most reliable way to catch instrumentation errors before acting on them.

The main product lesson: when users rate an experience highly but do not return, the question is not "how do we keep them" but "what are they actually using this for?" — which requires qualitative signal, not more dashboards.
